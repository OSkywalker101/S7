## 1. AI and Society: Framing the Relationship

### 1.1 Co-constitution, not one-way impact
AI and society **shape each other** (co-evolution):
- Society's data, rules, and values → shape AI (biased training, regulatory sandboxes, procurement).
- AI's decisions → reshape norms, markets, work, and power.

Dignum's loop: **Design → Decisions → Society → Design.** Each element feeds back.

### 1.2 Dual-use & societal pervasiveness
AI is a **general-purpose technology** (GPT-class): like electricity, it touches every domain. Pervasiveness raises cross-cutting societal questions:
- **Surveillance state:** facial recognition, predictive policing, social scoring (e.g., China's, or EU-prohibited social scoring in the AI Act).
- **Manipulation & misinformation:** deepfakes, personalized disinformation, opinion nudging (Cambridge Analytica; Susser on "invisible influence" of adaptive choice architectures).
- **Democracy & public discourse:** algorithmic echo chambers; automated bots in elections.
- **Health & welfare:** triage tools, diagnosis support, allocation of scarce resources — benefits vs. access equity.
- **Environment:** model-training carbon, e-waste, data-centre energy — but also optimization gains (course: AI *and Sustainability*).

> **Trick:** For "AI and society" answer, structure as a **2×2 matrix**: (Individual vs. Collective) × (Benefit vs. Harm), filled with examples (personalised medicine vs. surveillance; smart grids vs. energy waste).

## 2. Broader Societal Implications (systemic)

### 2.1 Power concentration
- Compute, data, and top talent concentrate in few corporations/States → **techno-oligopoly**.
- Consequences: single points of failure; regulatory capture; global "digital colonialism" (Birhane – extraction from the Global South, see Unit 4).
- Mitigations: public investment, open standards, data cooperatives, compute governance.

### 2.2 Meaning, dignity and human worth
- **Automation of judgement** (Zoller; Vallor): replacing humans in tasks that *build character/wisdom* (caregiving, teaching, soldiering) may strip opportunities for human flourishing.
- **Instrumentalisation risk:** being scored, ranked, surveilled reduces dignity (dehumanising effects; "people as data points" – deontology tie-in).
- **Deskilling:** over-reliance on AI tools erodes human skills (pilots' manual-flying decay, doctors' diagnostic-chart atrophy).

### 2.3 Privacy & autonomy at scale
- Pervasive data collection creates **informational asymmetry** between citizens and corporations/states.
- **Invisible influence** (Susser): AI design that steers choices (dark patterns, adaptive choice architectures) undermines autonomy even when "informed consent" boxes exist.

## 3. AI, Work and the Future of Labour (socio-economic core)

### 3.1 What AI does to jobs (three-part economic story — essential for exam answers)
1. **Automation/Displacement:** tasks previously done by humans are performed by machines → occupation disruption (routine cognitive + routine manual tasks at greatest risk). *But note: automatable *tasks*, not necessarily whole *jobs*; occupations are bundles of tasks.*
2. **Complementarity/Creation:** new tasks & new jobs arise (data labelling, prompt engineering, ML engineering, AI ethics/auditing) — historically net-positive "reallocation."
3. **Income & bargaining re-distribution:** returns to capital vs. labour; winner-take-most effects; gig-work micro-task exploitation (data-labour in the Global South).

### 3.2 Key readings/lenses (from the ethics handbook Week 4 — quote for depth)
- **James, "Planning for Mass Unemployment"** – argues we should plan well in advance for the possibility of persistently high unemployment (basic-income style policy planning).
- **Danaher, "Will life be worth living in a world without work?"** – old "meaning through work" thesis challenged: meaning can come from relationships, play, purpose.
- **Zoller, "Skilled perception, authenticity against automation"** – expertise/skill is infused into identity; automating skilled work de-authenticates it.
- **Vallor, "AI and the Automation of Wisdom"** – automation can out-skill individuals on *narrow* domains but risks automating away the *phronesis* (practical wisdom) domains — argue for keeping humans "in the loop" where judgement/virtue matter.

### 3.3 Policy responses (know at least four for "how should society respond")
1. **Reskilling/upskilling** & lifelong learning (wage subsidies for training).
2. **Portable-benefit & income floors** (universal basic income or negative income tax; universal basic services).
3. **Stronger labour protections** for platform/gig workers (algorithmic-transparency duties, right to explanation of management algorithms).
4. **Pro-competitive antitrust on data/compute markets.**
5. **Transition planning / just-transition funds** for affected communities ("planning for mass unemployment" – James).
6. **Public+private R&D investment** in augmentation & socially-useful AI (health, climate) as counterweight to pure displacement.

> **Balance-tip:** never write "AI destroys jobs, full stop." The evidence shows *task-level automation + occupational reallocation + creation of new work*, with distributional pain. A balanced, layered answer gets the marks.

## 4. Socio-Economic Impacts Beyond Jobs

### 4.1 Inequality dynamics
- **Skill- and capital-biased:** benefits flow disproportionately to already-skilled workers and tech capital owners → widened within- and between-country inequality.
- **Geographic concentration:** value creation clusters in a few hubs (Silicon Valley, Bangalore) while labour-intensive costs spread.

### 4.2 Access & the digital divide
- **Infrastructure divide:** compute, bandwidth, energy asymmetries.
- **Capability divide:** literacy, education, linguistic coverage (many LLMs weak on non-majority languages/dialects).
- **Trust divide:** marginalized groups mistrust biased systems → differential digital participation.
- **Impact:** "responsible AI in social context" requires distributing benefits & burdens fairly (procedural + distributive justice).

### 4.3 Economic externalities
- **Surveillance capitalism:** behavioural surplus monetisation — privacy as commodity.
- **Ballooned energy & resource use** (training GPT-scale models; data centres; water cooling) — *ties to the course name — Sustainability*. Also positive: AI-optimised energy grids, climate modelling (but lifecycle analysis matters).
- **Financial instability:** high-frequency algorithmic trading; AI-generated market manipulation; concentration of market power.

## 5. Superintelligence and Society (revisited at society level)

### 5.1 Broader societal implications
- **Allocation & governance of powerful AI** becomes a matter of *global* governance — no single nation governs advanced AI's compute/weights (gradient of the alignment problem, Unit 3).
- **Public-policy stance:** precautionary capabilities-gating (compute-thresholds, red-team reporting), international agreements (like nuclear non-proliferation analogies), and "safety research funding" as social hedge.
- **Democracy stakes:** misaligned superintelligence = deepest form of power concentration, decision-autonomy at planetary scale.

### 5.2 Responsible AI in social context: the meaning
Responsible AI **in social context** = considering how a system behaves *within and upon* social structures, not just in a lab: feedback loops, fairness over time, cultural specificity, distribution of benefits/burdens, and stability of institutions.

| Frame | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Responsible AI (Unit 2–3) | Is it lawful/ethical/robust? (artefact) |
| Responsible AI in social context | Does it *also* sustain society's values, equity, institutions in the long run? (embedded deployment) |

## 6. Public Policy Issues Related to Advanced AI Systems

### 6.1 Regulatory design tensions (the eternal triangle)
1. **Innovation vs. precaution:** over-regulation chills start-ups/labs; under-regulation causes harms (babysitter pattern: "move fast, patch later").
2. **National vs. global:** competing regulators → regulatory arbitrage; need international coordination + export/import controls on frontier AI.
3. **Sectoral vs. horizontal:** domain-specific rules (health, finance) vs. a general AI law (EU AI Act is horizontal).

### 6.2 Concrete public-policy instruments (memorize)
- **Standards & certification** (ISO/IEC 42001; conformity assessment).
- **Record-keeping / model registries** (high-risk AI registered; training-log mandates).
- **Impact assessments** (FRIA, DPIA-adjacent).
- **Liability rules** (product-liability reform for AI; EU AI Liability Directive).
- **Procurement preconditions** (public bodies require fairness/transparency).
- **Antitrust/data-sharing mandates** for foundational models.
- **International agreements** (UN advisory body; Council of Europe treaty).
- **Compute governance** — export controls, concentration thresholds, compute-use registration.

### 6.3 Democracy and civic engagement
- Public deliberation / citizen assemblies on AI (e.g., "AI awareness" campaigns, AI literacy curricula).
- Right to explanation & contestation (GDPR Art.22; AI Act transparency).
- Enforceable **redress** for algorithmic harms.

## 7. Quick Revision

### One-liners
- **AI–society:** co-constitutive; Design→Decisions→Society→Design loop.
- **Broader implications:** power concentration, surveillance, manipulation, dehumanisation, deskilling, environment.
- **Labour:** tasks→ displaced & created; income concentration; gig exploitation; reskill + income floor policies.
- **Inequality:** capital/skill-biased spread; digital divides (infrastructure, capability, trust).
- **Superintelligence/policy:** precaution, capability-gating, global governance, safety R&D.
- **Policy instruments:** standards, registries, impact assessments, liability, procurement, antitrust, compute controls.

### Likely exam questions
1. Critically evaluate the claim that "AI will create more jobs than it destroys."
2. Explain the socio-economic impacts of AI on inequality, with at least three policy responses.
3. What are "broader societal implications" of AI? Discuss power, surveillance and misinformation.
4. How should societies regulate advanced AI? Compare innovation-vs-precaution and national-vs-global tensions.
5. Discuss the AI narrative's influence on public policy (link Unit 4).
6. What does "responsible AI in social context" add beyond "responsible AI"?

### Tips & Tricks
- Open any socio-economic question with a **co-evolution** line and close with an **equity** line (distribute benefits & burdens fairly).
- Use *task vs. job* distinction boldly — it's the modern, evidence-based position.
- Cite one classic per sub-topic: **James** (unemployment planning), **Danaher** (meaning), **Zoller/Vallor** (skill/authenticity), **Birhane** (colonialism), **Susser** (manipulation), **Bostrom** (superintelligence).
- For exam diagrams: draw the **2×2 benefit/harm × individual/collective** matrix; draw the **regulation triangle** (innovation–precaution–global).
- Never end Unit 5 answers without the **policy instrument list** — it demonstrates practicality and is easy marks.
- Remember the course title: **AI Ethics *and Sustainability*** — mention environmental lifecycle in at least one answer.
